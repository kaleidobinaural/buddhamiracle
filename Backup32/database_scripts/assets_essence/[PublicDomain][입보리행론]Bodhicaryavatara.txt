Introduction & 용어 사전 (Glossary)
Concept: Bodhi (Awakening / Illumination)
Definition: The ultimate state of mental clarity and emotional liberation. It is not merely an intellectual understanding of reality, but a profound integration of deep wisdom and active compassion. In therapy, Bodhi represents the awakening of our innate capacity for healing, where we transition from a state of emotional confusion to a spacious, balanced awareness that seeks to heal both our own pain and the suffering of the world around us.
Concept: Caryā (Practice / The Journey)
Definition: The active, structured process of psychological and emotional training. Rather than viewing healing as a sudden, magical event, Caryā frames it as a step-by-step journey (or "march") across various stages of self-discipline. It represents the daily commitment to behavioral activation, mindfulness practices, and emotional exercises that gradually decondition our oldest, most destructive habits.
Concept: Bodhicitta (The Mind of Awakening / La pensée de la Bodhi)
Definition: The conscious intention and passionate drive to achieve emotional freedom not just for oneself, but to become a source of healing for all living beings. In modern psychology, Bodhicitta acts as the ultimate antidote to self-absorbed anxiety and depressive rumination. By shifting our core focus from "how do I save myself" to "how can I serve and heal others," we expand our cognitive boundaries, finding a profound, exhaustless source of resilience and purpose.
Concept: Mahāyāna (The Great Vehicle)
Definition: A psychological and relational framework built on the principle of collective, interconnected healing. Rather than seeking a private, isolated escape from life's distress (individualistic peace), the Mahāyāna perspective emphasizes that our personal well-being is deeply tied to the well-being of the collective. It represents a relational model of therapy where we build safe, co-regulating spaces of mutual support and universal empathy.
Concept: Pāramitā (Reaching the Other Shore / Perfection)
Definition: Reaching a state of complete emotional sovereignty and unshakeable resilience, described metaphorically as "arriving at the other shore." This is achieved through the systematic practice of six core virtues: generosity, ethical discipline, patience, enthusiastic energy, meditative concentration, and wisdom. These act as emotional stabilizers that protect the mind from being swept away by the stormy waters of reactive conditioning.
Concept: Mādhyamika (The Middle Way / Emptiness of the Ego)
Definition: The systematic deconstruction of the rigid, defensive construct of the "Ego" (le Moi). In cognitive therapy, this aligns with identifying and dissolving our reified beliefs about who we are. By realizing that our thoughts, emotions, and identities are "empty" of a solid, permanent, and independent core, we realize that we are fluid, ever-changing, and deeply interconnected, releasing us from the prison of identity-defense and self-criticism.
Concept: Interversion of Self and Other (La substitution du moi et d'autrui)
Definition: The advanced psychological practice of mentally exchanging places with another person. It is empathy taken to its highest level. By imagining oneself in the position of another, we see our own selfish defenses from the outside and experience their pain from the inside. This cognitive role-reversal transforms defensive ego-centrism into authentic, compassionate connection, resolving interpersonal conflicts at their root.
Concept: Purifying Virtue of Suffering (La vertu purifiante de la douleur)
Definition: The cognitive reframing of pain and adversity as experiential teachers rather than punishments or signs of failure. When faced with suffering, instead of reacting with anger or despair, we use the experience to build patience, soften our ego-arrogance, and develop deep empathy for others who are also hurting. It is a powerful framework for post-traumatic growth, turning our wounds into sources of profound wisdom and resilience.

CHAPTER 1
[CH1_P1]
Clean_Text: Having respectfully bowed to the Buddhas, their spiritual heirs, the Body of the Law, and all venerable guides, I shall briefly set forth, in accordance with tradition, the spiritual practice of the Bodhisattvas.
Context_Notes: This opening verse establishes a baseline of humility and reverence. "Their Fils" (Note 1) refers to the Bodhisattvas, those destined for awakening. "The Body of the Law" (Dharmakāya, Note 2) represents absolute reality or the ultimate nature of truth.
[CH1_P2]
Clean_Text: I have nothing to say that has not been said before, and I possess no literary skill. Thus, I write this not out of a desire to instruct others, but simply to purify and heal my own heart.
Context_Notes: In psychological terms, this is an act of self-soothing and expressive writing. The author acknowledges that writing serves as a contemplative tool for personal integration, prioritizing inner alignment over external validation.
[CH1_P3]
Clean_Text: Writing this will at least help increase the flow of devotion within me, supporting what is good. Yet, if someone else of a similar temperament should happen to read these words, they too may find some benefit.
Context_Notes: Expresses a gentle, open hope for shared resonance. When we write from a place of vulnerability and honest self-reflection, we naturally create a space where others who struggle with similar patterns can find solace.
[CH1_P4]
Clean_Text: This rich opportunity of human life is incredibly difficult to obtain, yet once found, it can fulfill every true human purpose. If we do not make use of it to cultivate the good now, how could we ever hope to find such a rare opportunity again?
Context_Notes: "The plenitude of the moment" (Note 3) refers to a rare alignment of favorable conditions (human birth, mental clarity). In modern terms, it is the realization of our present agency and the urgent need to break free from unconscious inertia while we can.
[CH1_P5]
Clean_Text: Just as a flash of lightning briefly cuts through the thick darkness of a cloudy night, so too, through the quiet influence of the Buddhas, does a wholesome thought occasionally flash through the human mind.
Context_Notes: Describes the fleeting nature of positive insights. Our habitual, negative patterns can feel like a thick, enveloping darkness. Wholesome thoughts or moments of clarity often feel like brief, sudden flashes of lightning that we must seize before they vanish.
[CH1_P6]
Clean_Text: Because our positive impulses are always fragile, while the force of destructive habits is vast and terrifying, what other good could possibly conquer them except the Mind of Awakening?
Context_Notes: "The Mind of Awakening" (Bodhicitta) is not just an intellectual idea, but a powerful emotional and cognitive engine. Our ordinary willpower is too weak to fight deep-seated patterns of trauma, greed, or anger on its own; we need a larger, altruistic purpose to transform them.
[CH1_P7]
Clean_Text: The supreme sages contemplated for countless ages, and in the end, they saw this single Good, which gathers joy and overflows to wash away the suffering of all living beings.
Context_Notes: This highlights the historical and empirical validation of Bodhicitta. Sages over "kalpas" (Note 4/5, cycles of time) analyzed human suffering and concluded that an active, compassionate focus on the welfare of others is the ultimate cure for existential pain.
[CH1_P8]
Clean_Text: Whoever wishes to cross over the endless sorrows of life, to dispel the pain of all living creatures, and to enjoy hundreds of peaceful joys, must never let go of the Mind of Awakening.
Context_Notes: Setting Bodhicitta as a non-negotiable anchor for mental health and resilience. It serves as a compass, guiding us through life's storms and keeping us connected to a larger, meaningful purpose.
[CH1_P9]
Clean_Text: The moment a person trapped in the prison of their own reactive conditioning awakens this Mind of Awakening, they are instantly proclaimed a child of the Buddhas, held in deep respect by both gods and humans.
Context_Notes: Radical self-redefinition. Instantly shifting from a "victim of circumstance" (trapped in the prison of existence) to a "child of the Buddhas." This psychological shift from helplessness to active agency and high self-worth is deeply empowering.
[CH1_P10]
Clean_Text: Taking this impure, fragile form of ours, it transforms it into an invaluable, indestructible vessel of diamond—a Buddha. Hold fast to this transformative elixir, which is called the Mind of Awakening.
Context_Notes: The metaphor of the "elixir" (Note 6) or philosopher's stone. In alchemy and therapy, this represents the integration and transformation of our messy, vulnerable, and wounded parts (the impure body) into a resilient, unshakeable state of wholeness (the diamond-body).
[CH1_P11]
Clean_Text: This jewel has been thoroughly tested and found to be of supreme value by the wise guides of humanity's long journey. Hold it fast, then, you who navigate these unpredictable markets of life and destiny.
Context_Notes: Metaphor of a trade market. In the chaotic transaction of worldly life, where we trade our time and energy for passing experiences, Bodhicitta is the only asset that holds permanent, appreciating value.
[CH1_P12]
Clean_Text: Just like a banana tree that dies once it has given its fruit, every other merit is eventually spent. But the Mind of Awakening is a tree that yields fruit continuously, producing more and more without ever being exhausted.
Context_Notes: Distinguishes between ordinary, selfish good deeds (which run out once we get the temporary reward we wanted) and altruistic, compassionate action (which is self-renewing, continuously feeding our emotional and spiritual growth).
[CH1_P13]
Clean_Text: Even one who has committed the most painful mistakes can find instant safety by relying upon it, just as one escapes a great danger through the protection of a brave hero. Why, then, do we remain so careless, refusing to take refuge in it?
Context_Notes: Self-forgiveness and rehabilitation. No matter how deep our past mistakes or trauma, aligning ourselves with compassion and the welfare of others acts as an instant psychological shield, giving us the safety and focus needed to rebuild our lives.
[CH1_P14]
Clean_Text: Like the fire that consumes the world at the end of an age, it burns away our greatest errors in a single moment. Its boundless benefits were revealed by the wise Maitreya to the seeker Sudhana.
Context_Notes: Reference to Note 7 (Gandavyûhasûtra). The imagery of fire represents the rapid, cathartic dissolution of guilt, shame, and past unwholesome conditioning through the sheer warmth and light of active compassion.
[CH1_P15]
Clean_Text: In brief, this Mind of Awakening has two aspects: the aspiration toward awakening, and the active journey toward it.
Context_Notes: Introduces the distinction between aspiring Bodhicitta (bodhipranidhicitta) and engaging Bodhicitta (bodhiprasthanacitta). This is a crucial distinction in behavioral therapy: wanting to heal vs. actively doing the work of healing.
[CH1_P16]
Clean_Text: The wise understand the difference between the two to be like the difference between a traveler who wishes to set out on a journey, and one who is already on the road.
Context_Notes: Translating intentions into actions. Wishing to be peaceful and compassionate (aspiring) is a beautiful start, but it only bears continuous fruit when we actually step onto the path and do the daily practice (engaging).
[CH1_P17]
Clean_Text: The wish for awakening brings beautiful fruits even in this world, yet it does not flow with the continuous, inexhaustible merit of actually being on the journey.
Context_Notes: Re-emphasizes the power of active commitment. While good intentions feel comforting, it is our active, daily choices and behavioral changes that yield robust, long-term psychological resilience.
[CH1_P18_P19]
Clean_Text: From the very moment your mind firmly embraces the unyielding resolve to liberate the boundless multitude of living beings, even when you are asleep or distracted, the flow of your positive energy increases continuously, expanding like the infinity of space.
Context_Notes: The unconscious power of a deep core value. When we align our identity with a vast, altruistic purpose, that core value runs in our psychological background, continuously shaping our perspective, even when we are not consciously focusing on it.
[CH1_P20]
Clean_Text: This truth was declared with clear proof by the Buddha himself in the teachings of the Subāhupṛicchā, for the benefit of those who still cling to a lesser ideal.
Context_Notes: Reference to Note 8. Encourages practitioners to step out of narrow, self-absorbed healing (the lesser ideal) and realize that expanding our circle of care is actually the most efficient way to heal ourselves.
[CH1_P21_P22]
Clean_Text: If someone who simply wishes to cure a few people of a headache acquires immense merit, how much greater is the goodness of one who desires to free all beings from infinite suffering and endow them with infinite qualities?
Context_Notes: A beautiful logic of scale. It invites us to see the immense therapeutic value of broad, unrestricted compassion. If localized kindness is healing, then universal compassion is a profound, life-altering mental state.
[CH1_P23]
Clean_Text: What mother or what father has ever carried such a generous wish? What god, what sage, or what holy teacher?
Context_Notes: Reference to Note 9 (rishis). This points out the unique, radical nature of the Bodhisattva ideal, which transcends ordinary, biological, or culturally restricted patterns of care.
[CH1_P24]
Clean_Text: None of them ever formed such a desire, even in their dreams, for themselves; how, then, could they have wished it for others?
Context_Notes: True altruism is rare and does not happen automatically. It requires conscious, deliberate training to move past our natural survival instincts and cultivate a mind that wishes absolute happiness for everyone.
[CH1_P25]
Clean_Text: This pearl among beings, this unprecedented jewel—how could it arise in ordinary minds, when others do not feel such concern even for their own welfare?
Context_Notes: The Bodhisattva is a rare psychological archetype. Ordinary minds are often too caught up in immediate anxieties and defense mechanisms to even consider their own long-term well-being, let alone that of others.
[CH1_P26]
Clean_Text: It is the ultimate source of the world's joy, the true medicine for the world's pain, a precious diamond of the spirit. How can we ever measure the boundless merit it holds?
Context_Notes: Re-framing compassion as a robust mental vaccine. It is the ultimate defense against depression and anxiety, protecting both our own mind and the minds of those we touch.
[CH1_P27]
Clean_Text: A simple wish for the welfare of the world is more precious than honoring the Buddha; how much more so when we actively join that wish with the effort to bring happiness to all?
Context_Notes: Practical compassion is superior to passive worship. The text prioritizes active, engaged help and systematic service over mere ritual or intellectual admiration of wisdom.
[CH1_P28]
Clean_Text: Humans throw themselves into suffering in their desperate attempt to escape suffering; in their craving for happiness, they foolishly destroy their own joy as if they were their own worst enemies.
Context_Notes: A profound observation of human self-sabotage (maladaptive coping). Out of ignorance, we chase happiness through means that cause pain (addiction, anger, control), acting as our own worst enemies.
[CH1_P29_P30]
Clean_Text: They are starved of happiness and tortured in a thousand ways. For the one who will satisfy them with all joy, cut through their pain, and dissolve their confusion—where can we find such a good person, such a friend, or such merit?
Context_Notes: The therapist and helper as a secure base. In a world full of pain and confusion, a truly compassionate, steady person acts as a vital sanctuary and guide for others.
[CH1_P31]
Clean_Text: We naturally praise someone who merely returns a favor. What, then, can we say of the Bodhisattva, who gives generously without ever being asked?
Context_Notes: Distinguishes between transactional kindness (giving only to get back) and unconditional love. True compassion does not wait for a request; it responds spontaneously to the presence of suffering.
[CH1_P32_P33]
Clean_Text: Anyone who offers a simple meal to a few people is praised as a saint, for giving a meager portion that sustains them for half a day. What, then, can be said of the one who gives to an infinite number of beings, for an infinite time, the satisfaction of their every desire—a gift that never runs dry as long as there are beings in space?
Context_Notes: A beautiful contrast showing the vastness of the Bodhisattva's scope. While physical charity is wonderful, providing the psychological and spiritual tools for self-liberation and permanent peace is the highest gift.
[CH1_P34]
Clean_Text: Anyone who harbors a harmful thought against such a generous host, the Bodhisattva, will remain in the dark realms of suffering for as many ages as that thought lasted. Thus has the Master declared.
Context_Notes: Shows the psychological feedback loop of resentment. Directing hatred toward someone who is completely dedicated to love and service creates a highly toxic, self-destructive state of mind that traps us in our own internal hells.
[CH1_P35]
Clean_Text: But anyone who turns their heart toward them with devotion will reap fruits far greater than their errors. While it takes effort to do harm to a Bodhisattva, doing good to them flows naturally and without effort.
Context_Notes: Wholesome connection is our natural baseline. When we align our minds with compassionate archetypes, our positive, healing mental habits naturally take over, outshining our past negative conditioning.
[CH1_P36]
Clean_Text: I bow to the bodies of the Bodhisattvas, within whom this jewel of a sublime Mind is born; I take refuge in these mines of happiness, whom we cannot even offend without receiving some blessing in return.
Context_Notes: Reference to Note 10. The ultimate expression of safety and forgiveness: a Bodhisattva's compassion is so unshakeable that even when we lash out at them in our pain, they respond only with love and healing, acting as a perfect, secure base.

CHAPTER 2
[CH2_P1]
Clean_Text: To win this precious jewel that is the Mind of Awakening, I pay homage to the Buddhas, to the pure Jewel of the Good Dharma, and to the Bodhisattvas, who are boundless oceans of spiritual merit.
Context_Notes: This begins the ritual of purification. Honoring the highest symbols of wisdom, truth, and compassion (the Three Jewels) helps shift the mind's focus from self-absorption to a state of humble receptivity.
[CH2_P2_P6]
Clean_Text: All the flowers, fruits, and healing herbs, all the treasures of the universe, all pure and delicious waters, the mountains made of precious gems, the quiet beauty of the wild forests, the flowering vines, the trees whose branches bend under the weight of sweet fruit, the fragrances of both human and divine realms, the wish-fulfilling trees and jewel-laden trees, the lakes decorated with lotuses and echoing with the song of swans, all wild and cultivated plants, and all the beautiful ornaments spread throughout the vastness of space—all these things that belong to no one, I gather them in my mind and offer them as a gift to the Great Sages and their spiritual heirs. May they accept them, they who are worthy of the most beautiful offerings; may they have compassion on me, they who are the Great Compassionate Ones!
Context_Notes: The practice of mental offering (pūjā). By mentally offering the unowned beauties of the natural world (Note 11), we cultivate a sense of abundance and non-clinging generosity, training the mind to appreciate beauty without the desire to possess it.
[CH2_P7]
Clean_Text: I am without merit and deeply impoverished; I have nothing else of value to offer. May the Protectors, whose only thought is the welfare of others, accept these mental gifts for my own healing, through the power of their presence!
Context_Notes: Acknowledges one's vulnerability and emotional poverty. Expressing this humility is a powerful psychological release, letting go of the exhausting pretense of having it all together and inviting healing.
[CH2_P8]
Clean_Text: I give myself completely and without reserve to the Awakened Ones and to their spiritual heirs. Accept me into your service, sublime beings; with deep devotion, I offer myself to you.
Context_Notes: "Jina" (Vainqueurs, Note 12) is a synonym for the Buddha. Offering oneself to the path represents a radical commitment to self-transcendence, choosing to live in alignment with compassionate values rather than selfish desires.
[CH2_P9]
Clean_Text: Accepted into your service, I am now free from fear. I shall work for the ultimate welfare of all beings; I shall escape my past mistakes and commit no new harms.
Context_Notes: Surrendering to a larger, compassionate purpose brings a profound psychological relief, quieting existential anxiety and fear. It marks a clear commitment to behavioral change and ethical boundaries.
[CH2_P10_P11]
Clean_Text: In beautiful, fragrant bathhouses decorated with jewel-encrusted columns, shimmering pearl curtains, and floors of pure, brilliant crystal, I prepare a bath for the Buddhas and their spiritual heirs, using urns of noble gems filled with sweet water and fresh flowers, accompanied by the gentle sounds of songs and music.
Context_Notes: A vivid, sensory-rich visualization practice designed to calm the nervous system. Mentally bathing the images of the Buddhas represents purifying our own consciousness, washing away the emotional dirt of anxiety and self-doubt.
[CH2_P12]
Clean_Text: With peerless, soft cloths, scented with incense and washed clean of all stains, I gently dry their bodies and clothe them in bright, beautifully fragrant robes.
Context_Notes: Visualization of gentle care and respect. This acts as a somatic metaphor for treating ourselves and others with extreme gentleness, respect, and emotional care.
[CH2_P13]
Clean_Text: With celestial garments, soft, fine, and radiant, and with various beautiful ornaments, I adorn Samantabhadra, Ajita, Mañjughosha, Lokeśvara, and all the other Bodhisattvas.
Context_Notes: Adorning the archetypal figures of compassion and wisdom (Note 13). By mentally dressing these symbols of our own highest potential in beautiful garments, we reinforce our deep respect for these qualities.
[CH2_P14]
Clean_Text: With exquisite perfumes whose fragrance fills the entire universe, I anoint the bodies of all the Buddhas, which shine like pure, polished, and lustrous gold.
Context_Notes: Sensory visualization designed to evoke feelings of warmth, richness, and deep safety in our internal world.
[CH2_P15]
Clean_Text: With the most beautifully fragrant flowers—jasmine, blue lotuses, and sweet blossoms—and with delicately crafted garlands, I honor the deeply adorable Buddhas.
Context_Notes: Honoring wisdom and purity with the gentle, fleeting beauty of flowers, representing our appreciation for the transient beauty of life.
[CH2_P16]
Clean_Text: I offer them clouds of rich, deep incense that delight the heart with their sweet fragrance, and I present them with various pure foods and refreshing drinks.
Context_Notes: Offering nourishment. In our internal space, this represents feeding our highest aspirations and positive qualities with wholesome mental inputs.
[CH2_P17]
Clean_Text: I offer them jewel-encrusted lamps resting on golden lotuses, and along the fragrant, polished floors, I scatter a beautiful path of fresh blossoms.
Context_Notes: Bringing light and beauty into our internal world. Lighting the path for the Buddhas is a metaphor for bringing clarity and mindfulness into the dark corners of our mind.
[CH2_P18]
Clean_Text: I present to these Merciful Ones beautiful, airy palaces decorated with shimmers of pearls, ringing with melodious hymns, and gracing the horizons with their beauty.
Context_Notes: Creating a vast, spacious, and harmonious internal palace. This helps the mind step out of its narrow, claustrophobic anxieties and rest in a wide, beautiful cognitive space.
[CH2_P19]
Clean_Text: I present to the Great Sages tall, elegant parasols of precious gems with golden handles, beautifully inlaid with pearls and shining with a radiant light.
Context_Notes: The parasol is a traditional symbol of protection. In psychological terms, it represents creating a secure, protective space of mindfulness that shields us from the harsh winds of emotional reactivity.
[CH2_P20]
Clean_Text: And now, let them rise—the delightful clouds of offerings, and the beautiful songs and music that bring joy to all living beings!
Context_Notes: Cultivating a mind that is full of spacious, celebratory, and generous energy, wishing that our internal peace overflows to bring joy to others.
[CH2_P21]
Clean_Text: May a continuous rain of flowers, jewels, and precious things fall upon the Good Dharma, the stupas, and the sacred images!
Context_Notes: Keeping our connection to the teachings (Note 14) and paths of wisdom fresh, alive, and constantly nourished with deep respect and appreciation.
[CH2_P22]
Clean_Text: Just as Mañjughosha and the other Bodhisattvas offer their devotion to the Awakened Ones, so do I honor the protecting Buddhas and their spiritual heirs.
Context_Notes: Aligning our practice with the great lineages of compassion. We do not practice in isolation; we join a long, supportive stream of human healing.
[CH2_P23]
Clean_Text: Through songs that are vast oceans of harmonious rhythms, I praise these oceans of spiritual merit. May these clouds of peaceful melody rise toward them without ever losing their way!
Context_Notes: Using sound, art, and expression to channel our positive emotional energy, reinforcing our connection to wholesome qualities.
[CH2_P24]
Clean_Text: As many times as there are atoms in all the worlds, I bow down before the Buddhas of the past, present, and future, before the Dharma, and before the Community.
Context_Notes: Radical humility. Prostrating before the Three Jewels (Note 15/16) helps dissolve the rigid, defensive walls of the ego, opening the heart to deep healing.
[CH2_P25]
Clean_Text: I honor all the sacred stupas and the dwellings of the Bodhisattvas; I bow with deep respect to my spiritual guides and all venerable practitioners.
Context_Notes: Acknowledging and respecting the spaces, communities, and mentors who facilitate our healing and growth.
[CH2_P26]
Clean_Text: I take refuge in the Buddha until I reach the essence of awakening; I take refuge in the Dharma and in the host of the Bodhisattvas.
Context_Notes: The formal act of taking refuge (Note 17). It means choosing where we look for ultimate safety: not in fleeting distractions or toxic defenses, but in wisdom, truth, and the community of compassion.
[CH2_P27]
Clean_Text: I turn to the Buddhas who dwell in every direction and to the deeply compassionate Bodhisattvas, and I speak to them with my hands joined in reverence.
Context_Notes: This marks the transition to the formal confession of sins (pâpadeçanâ, Note 18). It is the act of stepping into a safe, non-judgmental container of absolute compassion to look honestly at our shadow.
[CH2_P28_P29]
Clean_Text: All the harm I have done or caused others to do, like a foolish beast, throughout the endless cycle of my lives or in this present life—all the mistakes I have approved of in my blindness, leading to my own ruin—I now confess them all, burned with deep remorse.
Context_Notes: Radical self-honesty. Acknowledging our past unwholesome actions and habits without defensiveness. Remorse is a healthy, constructive pain that motivates change, unlike toxic guilt which paralyzes us.
[CH2_P30_P31]
Clean_Text: All the offenses I have committed by disrespecting the Three Jewels, my father, my mother, or any others who deserved my respect, whether in my actions, my speech, or my thoughts—all the harmful mistakes I have made, as a person burdened with so many flaws, I confess them all to you, oh Guides!
Context_Notes: Cleanses the psychological burden of past relational harms. Unexpressed guilt and unconfessed harms (Note 19) fester in the unconscious, causing chronic anxiety and depression. Bringing them to light dissolves their power.
[CH2_P33]
Clean_Text: How can I escape the consequences of my mistakes? Come quickly to my aid, and save me! May death not arrive too quickly, before my mind has been purified and healed!
Context_Notes: (Verse 32 is omitted as an interpolation in the source text, Note 20). Expresses the urgency of emotional healing. We must do the work of resolving our inner conflicts and regrets now, rather than putting it off.
[CH2_P34]
Clean_Text: Death does not wait to ask if your life's work is finished or unfinished. It strikes when we least expect it, catching us off guard. Let no one trust in their health or youth; death strikes like a sudden flash of lightning.
Context_Notes: A stark reminder of impermanence. Denying our mortality keeps us stuck in shallow, distractive habits. Accepting that our time is limited gives us the clarity and urgency to prioritize what truly matters.
[CH2_P35]
Clean_Text: My blind search for pleasure and my fear of pain have been constant triggers for my mistakes, forgetting that one day I must leave everything behind and depart.
Context_Notes: This identifies the core psychological trap: our automatic reactivity to pleasure and pain (the hedonic treadmill). We act destructively when we are desperately grasping for pleasure or frantically running from discomfort.
[CH2_P36]
Clean_Text: Those who anger me will cease to exist; those who please me will cease to exist; I myself will cease to exist, and absolutely nothing will remain.
Context_Notes: A powerful cognitive tool to de-escalate emotional reactivity. When we realize that both our enemies and our loved ones are transient, our anger and desperate grasping naturally soften.
[CH2_P37]
Clean_Text: The things I perceive today will become nothing but a passing memory, like a dream that vanishes and can never be seen again.
Context_Notes: Compares our current experience to a dream. Realizing the fluid, ungraspable nature of our experiences helps us hold our daily dramas with a lighter, more spacious touch.
[CH2_P38]
Clean_Text: While I still linger in this world, so many others have already departed, both friends and enemies. Yet the psychological burden of the mistakes I made because of them remains, standing threateningly before me.
Context_Notes: The persistence of psychological baggage. The people who triggered our anger or greed may be gone, but the negative mental conditioning we built remains inside us, continuing to affect our mental health.
[CH2_P39]
Clean_Text: I am merely a brief traveler passing through this earth, yet I have completely failed to understand this. Blinded by confusion, attachment, and hatred, I have committed so many mistakes.
Context_Notes: The cost of existential denial. When we forget that we are temporary travelers, we let our egoic defenses (attachment, hatred, confusion) run our lives, causing pain to ourselves and others.
[CH2_P40]
Clean_Text: Day and night, without a single moment's pause, my life force is spent, and nothing can ever buy it back. Is it not absolutely certain that I will die?
Context_Notes: Time is our most precious, non-renewable resource. Contemplating the unstoppable flow of time shakes us out of our complacency and encourages us to practice mindfulness today.
[CH2_P41]
Clean_Text: Very soon, lying here upon my bed surrounded by my loved ones, I will have to endure the lonely physical and emotional agony of my departure completely alone.
Context_Notes: We must face our ultimate transitions alone. Developing inner strength, emotional self-sufficiency, and a clean conscience is the only true preparation for these existential moments.
[CH2_P42]
Clean_Text: When we are seized by the silent messengers of death, what can parents or friends do to save us? At that moment, only the inner wealth of our wholesome actions can protect us—yet I have neglected to practice it.
Context_Notes: Relational and material security have limits. At the end of life, our money, status, and relationships cannot protect us from our own internal state (Note 21). Only our cultivated peace and kindness can support us.
[CH2_P43]
Clean_Text: Through my blind attachment to this fleeting life, my ignorance of danger, and my sheer carelessness, I have done so much harm, oh Protectors!
Context_Notes: Facing the painful reality of our past carelessness. This honest confession is the necessary first step to break through our denial and commit to a more conscious, mindful way of living.
[CH2_P44]
Clean_Text: A condemned man being led away to have a limb severed is frozen with terror; his throat is parched, his eyes are weak, and he can barely recognize the world around him.
Context_Notes: A vivid psychological description of panic and helplessness. It illustrates how physical and emotional terror can completely overwhelm our sensory perception and cognitive clarity.
[CH2_P45]
Clean_Text: What then will it be like when the cold terrors of death take hold of me, while I am consumed by fear, burning with fever, and soiled by my own helplessness?
Context_Notes: Facing the raw, somatic reality of dying. Preparing the mind through meditation helps us maintain stability and presence even in the face of intense physical collapse and fear.
[CH2_P46]
Clean_Text: My terrified eyes will search in every direction for some source of safety. What loving being will come to rescue me from this immense, overwhelming peril?
Context_Notes: The desperate search for safety. It highlights our deep need for a "secure base"—an internal sense of safety and connection that we can rely upon during life's greatest crises.

Context_Notes: The self-justification of greed. We often justify our frantic search for wealth by claiming we will use it for good, yet the anger, stress, and dishonesty we practice to get that wealth ruin our mental health.
[CH6_P61]

Context_Notes: Redefining survival. Survival is not just about keeping the physical body breathing; it is about preserving our capacity for joy, peace, and meaningful connection.

Clean_Text: You claim you hate your defamer because their words cause others to lose respect for you; why then, my heart, do you not feel the same anger when you see others being defamed?

[CH6_P63]
Clean_Text: You readily forgive those who are cold to you because of another's gossip; yet you refuse to forgive the gossip themselves, who is merely reacting out of their own toxic passions!
Context_Notes: The inconsistency of our resentment. We blame the messenger or the source of gossip, forgetting that they too are simply sick and reacting out of their own internal confusion.
[CH6_P64]
Clean_Text: Those who destroy or deface sacred images, stupas, or even the doctrine itself, do not deserve my hatred; for the Awakened Sages do not suffer from their actions.
Context_Notes: Protecting our peace from ideological outrage. Even when our most sacred values or symbols are attacked, falling into hatred only poisons our own mind. Wisdom and truth do not need our angry, reactive defense.
[CH6_P65]
Clean_Text: If someone harms our teachers, our parents, or those we love, let us quiet our anger, reminding ourselves that their actions are the natural, mechanical result of their own conditioning.
Context_Notes: Extending patience to relational pain. When our loved ones are harmed, we must set firm, protective boundaries, but carrying burning hatred for the perpetrator only adds more suffering to our own lives.

Clean_Text: The suffering experienced by living beings is always the result of some cause, whether conscious or unconscious. Since suffering can only be felt by a conscious mind, let us support and bear it with courage, my heart!

[CH6_P67]
Clean_Text: Some act out of confusion and cause harm; others, also confused, react with fierce anger. Who among them can we truly call innocent, and who is guilty?
Context_Notes: The shared tragedy of confusion. Both the perpetrator and the reactive victim are caught in the same tragic cycle of unconscious reactivity, calling for universal compassion rather than judgment.

Context_Notes: Choosing to be a source of harmony. Instead of participating in conflict, we choose to use our agency to cultivate and radiate kindness, helping to co-regulate our entire social environment.

Context_Notes: Cognitive projection of consequences. If we cannot even handle a minor insult today, how can we expect to survive the massive internal torment and broken life that chronic anger creates?

Clean_Text: Through my past anger, I have thrown myself thousands of times into states of deep, internal misery, and what did I gain? Absolutely nothing for myself, and nothing for others.

[CH6_P75]
Clean_Text: The temporary discomfort of practicing patience, however, is far lighter and is the source of boundless inner peace. We must rejoice in a discomfort that serves to dissolve the suffering of the entire world.
Context_Notes: Purposeful endurance. The discomfort we feel when practicing patience is a clean, healing pain that actively dismantles our negative conditioning and contributes to collective peace.
[CH6_P76]
Clean_Text: There are those who take great delight in praising the beautiful qualities of others; why, my heart, do you not find the same joy in celebrating them?

Context_Notes: The social benefits of appreciative joy. Sincere appreciation dissolves interpersonal barriers, establishing a safe, connected, and highly supportive relational field.

[CH6_P85]

Context_Notes: Exposes the extreme, destructive wishes of envy, which would rather see the entire world impoverished than see someone else succeed, showing how toxic unchecked envy is to our own mind.
[CH6_P86]
Clean_Text: Not only do you fail to grieve for your own destructive actions, but you actually feel jealous of those who are actively doing good!

Clean_Text: And for the sake of fame, people willingly sacrifice their wealth and risk their lives. Are those words of praise edible? Once you have departed this life, will you taste a single drop of that pleasure?

[CH7_P1]

[CH7_P40]

[CH7_P41]
Clean_Text: Physical pain, mental anguish, constant dangers, and the ultimate shattering of all their desires—this is the natural harvest of those who continue to sow seeds of harm.
Context_Notes: A psychological description of the feedback loop of negative behavior. Living destructively naturally results in anxiety, broken relationships, and internal chaos.

CHAPTER 9
[CH9_P1]
Clean_Text: All these auxiliary virtues are taught by the Sage for the sake of wisdom. Therefore, one must cultivate wisdom if they desire the complete ending of suffering.
Context_Notes: Wisdom (prajñā) is the ultimate goal of the entire Bodhisattva path. The previous chapters (charity, moral discipline, patience, energy, concentration) are preparatory phases designed to purify and prepare the mind for this absolute realization.
[CH9_P2]
Clean_Text: There are two truths: the relative truth of appearance, and the absolute truth of reality. Absolute reality lies beyond the reach of the conceptual mind, for the mind itself belongs to the realm of relative appearances.
Context_Notes: This introduces the Madhyamaka Two Truths doctrine: relative truth (samvriti-satya) which functions conceptually, and absolute truth (paramartha-satya) which is non-conceptual, direct insight into the emptiness of all phenomena.
[CH9_P3_P4]
Clean_Text: Correspondingly, there are two kinds of people: the meditative contemplatives and ordinary worldly people. The contemplatives see deeper than ordinary people, and they form a hierarchy based on the clarity of their insight. Yet, both groups can communicate through shared metaphors to reach the ultimate goal.
Context_Notes: Distinguishes between worldly perception and meditative insight (yogis vs. ordinary beings). Although they perceive differently, language and everyday analogies are used by the wise as a bridge to guide others toward non-conceptual truth.
[CH9_P5]
Clean_Text: Ordinary people perceive things as solidly real, rather than as fleeting illusions. This is the fundamental difference in perspective between worldly minds and contemplatives.
Context_Notes: Worldly minds suffer because of a cognitive bias that attributes permanent, intrinsic reality to changing, dependent phenomena (reification). Contemplatives view all things as empty and dream-like.
[CH9_P6]
Clean_Text: Sensations and physical forms seem real through common sense, not because of logical proof. But common sense is mistaken, just as it mistakenly judges an impure body to be pure.
Context_Notes: Common sense and physical perception are unreliable guides to ultimate reality. They are bound by cognitive illusions, such as mistaking transient, composite bodies for pure, permanent, desirable entities.
[CH9_P7]
Clean_Text: The Master taught the existence of objects merely as an introduction to emptiness, not as the absolute truth. If objects were truly momentary, as some believe, this momentariness would still belong to the relative realm, not the ultimate.
Context_Notes: Refuting the Abhidharma (early Buddhist) view of momentary atomic dhammas. Even momentariness is a conceptual construct used as a stepping stone to help the mind transition toward complete emptiness (śūnyatā).
[CH9_P8]
Clean_Text: There is no error in the relative truth as long as it aligns with insight. Compared to ordinary minds, the contemplatives see closer to the truth. For instance, even in common life, you deviate from the masses by recognizing that the physical form is fundamentally impure.
Context_Notes: Relative truth is valid within its functional context. The meditative view of physical impurity, while still relative, is closer to the truth because it counteracts the destructive delusion of physical attachment.
[CH9_P9_P10]
Clean_Text: "If all things are empty, then the Buddha is merely an illusion. What merit can come from honoring him?" The merit is exactly the same as if his existence were solidly real. "But if a being is merely an illusion, how can they die and be reborn?" An illusion lasts as long as the causes that produce it; that an illusion appears as a continuous stream does not mean it exists in reality.
Context_Notes: Addresses the nihilistic misunderstanding of emptiness. Even though the Buddha is conceptually "empty" of independent existence, honoring him produces positive psychological and karmic effects because the causal stream of relative reality still functions.
[CH9_P11]
Clean_Text: "If it is all an illusion, then there is no sin in killing an illusory human, since they have no real consciousness." No, because the act is committed against a being who appears to have consciousness; therefore, relative merit and demerit are still generated.
Context_Notes: Emptiness does not mean moral nihilism. Ethics operate fully within the realm of relative truth, where suffering and intention are functionally real and carry real psychological consequences.
[CH9_P12_P13a]
Clean_Text: "A conscious illusion is impossible; even magic cannot create a mind." But illusions take many forms and arise from different conditions; a single cause does not have to produce every kind of illusion.
Context_Notes: Defending the possibility of empty, illusory consciousness. Just as a physical illusion is born of visual trickery, conscious experience is born of mental conditioning.
[CH9_P13b_P15a]
Clean_Text: "If beings exist only as illusions, then does the Buddha also transmigrate?" As long as the causes of an illusion are not cut, the illusion continues to appear; but once the causes are cut, the illusion ceases, even in the relative realm.
Context_Notes: Transmigration (samsara) is a continuous stream of illusory conditioning. Nirvana is achieved not by destroying a real self, but by cutting the causes (craving, ignorance) that keep the illusion going.
[CH9_P15b_P16a]

[CH9_P16b_P18a]

Context_Notes: Refutes self-cognizing consciousness (svasamvedana). Consciousness cannot be its own object, just as a sword cannot cut its own blade or a finger cannot touch its own tip.
[CH9_P18b_P19a]

Context_Notes: This dismantles the concept of an intrinsically real, self-aware mind. Every phenomenon, including the mind, exists interdependently; nothing exists in and of itself.

[CH9_P27]

[CH9_P157_P158]

[CH9_P159_P161]

[CH9_P162_P163]

[CH9_P164_P165]

[CH9_P166]

[CH9_P167]

Context_Notes: A beautiful, compassionate aspiration. The realization of emptiness does not make us cold or detached; it fills us with an urgent, loving desire to share this liberating peace with a suffering world.

[CH10_P1]
Clean_Text: By the virtue I have accumulated through composing this Guide to the Bodhisattva Way, may all human beings adorn themselves with the compassionate practice of the Bodhisattvas!
Context_Notes: This is the formal act of Dedication (pariṇāmanā). It is a vital practice in Buddhist psychology to redirect any personal merit, pride, or achievement outward for the welfare of others, preventing the ego from co-opting spiritual progress.
[CH10_P2]
Clean_Text: May all those in every corner of the world who suffer from physical or mental pain obtain vast oceans of comfort, joy, and peace through my merit!

[CH10_P3]

[CH10_P4]

Context_Notes: Sukhāvatī is the Amitabha's Pure Land (Note 50), symbolizing a state of mind free from fear, distress, and trauma. The wish is to instantly transition minds in torment to a state of absolute psychological safety.
